Can Behavioral Signals Predict Content Decline?

A Machine-Learning Approach to Content Refresh Prioritization

Vinayak Suresh Menon · FlyRank ML Internship, Lane 2: Refresh Scoring
August 2026 · Repository

Abstract.

Content teams managing large portfolios face a recurring question: which pages should be refreshed first? Using 30,000 pseudonymized content pages from 32 clients (trailing 90-day behavioral metrics), we trained a decision-tree classifier to predict impression-trend decline—a proxy for editorial attention priority. On a client-holdout evaluation (clients unseen during training), the model achieved Precision@50 of 0.68 (base rate 0.39), a 1.7× lift over random and a 2.8× lift over a hand-crafted rule baseline (P@50 = 0.24). The output is a ranked refresh queue with transparent reason codes and action labels, designed as a human-reviewed decision-support tool—not an automated publishing system.

1. Introduction & Problem Statement

Content portfolios at scale decay silently. Pages that once ranked well lose impressions as competitors publish, algorithms update, and user intent shifts. An editorial team reviewing thousands of pages manually cannot identify which ones need attention most urgently.

This project frames that editorial decision as a classification problem: given a page's behavioral signals (search impressions, click-through rate, engagement metrics, content age, position), can we predict whether its impression trend is declining? A "yes" prediction doesn't mean the page will improve if refreshed—that would require an A/B test—but it surfaces the pages most likely to benefit from human review.

Unit of analysis: one content page.
Output: a ranked queue with scores, reason codes, and action labels.
Action: a content editor reviews the flagged page, confirms the signal, and decides whether to refresh.
Cost of a wrong call: wasted editorial time (≈2–6 hours per false positive); the model's 32% false-positive rate in top-50 is the reason human review is mandatory.

The dataset is the FlyRank ML Internship anonymized starter release: 30,000 rows × 44 columns, one row per pseudonymized content page, covering 32 pseudonymized clients. All metrics are aggregated over a trailing 90-day window ending at export time.

What was excluded and why

  • trend_direction and trend_pct — the label is derived from these; using them as features is direct leakage.
  • content_id and client_id — pseudonymous identifiers used only for grouping and splits, never as features.
  • provider_used and model_used — LLM provenance columns excluded from the model feature set (they describe how content was generated, not its performance).
  • No client names, URLs, domains, or raw queries appear anywhere in this work—all data is pseudonymized at source.

Data gotchas documented and handled: rate columns are ×100 percentages (CTR = 0.76 means 0.76%); avg_position = 0 means "no data" (1,205 rows); scroll_rate can exceed 100 (different measurement systems); missingness follows content_type (keyword-context columns blank for non-keyword articles).

3. Methodology

Label definition

is_declining_label = 1 when trend_direction == "down" (impression change < −20% between the most recent 30 days and the prior 30 days). This is a proxy for "would editorial attention help?"—not a direct measure of content quality.

Feature engineering

The preparation step fills blanks (numerics → 0, categoricals → "unknown") and adds log-transformed traffic columns (log_impressions_90d, log_clicks_90d, log_sessions_90d, log_ai_sessions_90d) plus binary indicators (has_clicks, has_ai_sessions, measurable_opportunity). The final feature vector contains 18 numeric features and 8 categorical features (one-hot encoded).

Baseline

A transparent, hand-crafted rule score combining four sub-scores:

  • Visibility score (40%): percentile rank of log-impressions
  • Freshness risk score (30%): percentile rank of days-since-last-update
  • Position opportunity score (25%): normalized position × visibility (for pages with valid position data)
  • Depth gap score (5%): inverse word-count percentile × visibility

Models trained

  • Logistic Regression — linear baseline
  • Decision Tree (max depth=5) — interpretable, shallow tree
  • Random Forest (n=200 estimators) — ensemble for comparison

Validation design

Client-holdout split: ~20% of clients (not rows) are held out entirely, so no client's pages appear in both training and test sets. This tests true cross-client generalization—the hardest realistic test for this data.

Leakage checks

  • Programmatic assertion: zero forbidden columns (trend_direction, trend_pct) in the feature set
  • Injection attack test: deliberately injecting a leaked feature produces near-perfect scores (confirming the detection mechanism works)
  • Both checks passed ✅ (documented in w06_validation_audit.ipynb)

5. Limitations & Honest Framing

Cross-sectional, not causal. This analysis observes associations between behavioral signals and declining trends. It does not establish that refreshing a flagged page will reverse the decline. An A/B test (refresh vs. no-refresh on matched pages) would be needed to establish causation.
One snapshot in time. The model was trained on a single 90-day window. Seasonal effects, algorithm updates, or market shifts may change which signals matter. The queue is a point-in-time recommendation.
32 clients only. Generalization to clients with very different content profiles (e.g., e-commerce product pages vs. B2B SaaS thought leadership) has not been tested. New clients should be validated before applying the queue.
No content-quality features. The model uses behavioral and structural signals (impressions, position, age, CTR). It cannot assess whether the content is factually accurate, well-written, or topically relevant.
Label is a proxy. is_declining_label is defined as impression trend direction = "down" (>20% decline). Impression decline doesn't always mean the content needs refreshing—seasonality, product sunsetting, or intentional deprioritization are all valid reasons for decline.
False-positive rate in top-50: 32%. 16 of the top 50 predictions are false positives. This is why human review is mandatory before acting.

Starting point: 3,576 pages are flagged as high confidence (top-20% score + 500+ impressions + 10+ sessions + model probability ≥ 0.50). These are the recommended first batch for a team sprint.

What should never be automated

  • Publishing a refresh without human review — the model scores signals, not content quality
  • Deleting or redirecting pages based on model scores alone — irreversible, with 32% FP rate
  • Promising clients that refresh "will improve rankings" — no causal evidence
  • Applying the queue to new clients without re-validation

Monitoring and retrain triggers

  • Re-generate the queue when data is > 90 days old
  • Re-validate if the base rate shifts > 5 percentage points
  • Re-train if Precision@50 drops below 0.50 on new data
  • Re-train after major search algorithm updates
